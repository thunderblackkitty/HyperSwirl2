Discover Our Purpose
01 / The beginning

Where does a cyclone come from?

A cyclone does not appear suddenly. It develops when several atmospheric and ocean conditions work together over warm tropical waters.

A tropical cyclone is a large rotating weather system with a low-pressure centre and organised thunderstorms. It usually begins as a cluster of showers and thunderstorms over a warm ocean.

Warm seawater provides heat and moisture. As moist air rises, the air pressure near the surface becomes lower. More air flows towards this low-pressure area, and under the right conditions the system begins to organise and rotate.

Remember: Warm water alone cannot create a cyclone. The atmosphere also needs moisture, a pre-existing disturbance, enough spin and relatively weak vertical wind shear.

02 / Ingredients

Conditions needed for formation

These ingredients make cyclone development possible, although even favourable conditions do not guarantee that a cyclone will form.

01

Warm ocean water

Sea-surface temperatures of about 26.5°C or higher provide heat and moisture that help fuel thunderstorms.

02

Moist air

Moisture in the lower and middle atmosphere helps clouds and thunderstorms continue developing.

03

Low pressure disturbance

A pre-existing area of unsettled weather gives air a place to converge and rise. Cyclones do not form from nothing.

04

Earth's rotation

The Coriolis effect helps organise the air into a rotating circulation. Cyclones rarely form very close to the equator.

05

Unstable atmosphere

Air that can keep rising encourages strong thunderstorm clouds to grow vertically.

06

Low wind shear

Winds should not change too sharply in speed or direction with height, so the storm can stay organised.

03 / Step by step

How a cyclone develops

Follow the sequence from the first unsettled clouds to a mature storm — and finally to its weakening stage.

Stage 01 Tropical disturbance

Clusters of showers and thunderstorms form.

Stage 02 Tropical depression

A more organised low-pressure circulation develops.

Stage 03 Tropical storm / cyclone

Winds strengthen and the system becomes more organised.

Stage 04 Mature then weaken

The storm reaches peak strength, then loses energy.

The sun warms the ocean. Water evaporates and adds moisture to the air. Warm, moist air rises, leaving lower pressure near the ocean surface. More air begins moving towards this area.
As the rising air cools, water vapour condenses into cloud droplets. Condensation releases heat into the atmosphere, helping air rise and supporting further thunderstorm development.
Air flows towards the low-pressure centre. Earth's rotation helps the circulation turn. Thunderstorms begin organising around the centre instead of remaining as separate clusters.
If warm water, moisture and favourable winds continue, the pressure may fall and the winds may become stronger. Curved rainbands can form around the centre.
In a well-developed cyclone, a relatively calm eye may form at the centre. The eyewall around it contains some of the strongest winds and heaviest rainfall. Not every cyclone has a clearly visible eye.
When a cyclone moves over land or cooler water, it is cut off from much of its warm, moist ocean energy. Friction over land and unfavourable atmospheric conditions can also disrupt its circulation, causing it to weaken.
Important: The exact naming and wind-speed thresholds used to classify a cyclone differ between meteorological agencies and ocean basins. The stages above explain the general development process.
